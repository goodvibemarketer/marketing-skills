## Post version 2 (longer, more narrative, link in post)

Two years running a weekly newsletter alongside a consultancy has taught me one thing.

The job most newsletter operators think they have is not the job that scales.

The assumption is that creators create. Writers write. Whatever you publish this week, you produced it by hand, from scratch, in your own time. It is a flattering image. It is also a trap.

The trap is that writing does not scale linearly with effort. Double the hours, you do not double the output, because the bottleneck is cognitive load rather than time. The writer runs out of energy before they run out of ideas. Eventually quality drops, cadence drops, or the writer quits.

I hit that wall earlier this year. Rather than reduce the cadence or accept lower quality, I did something different. I separated the job.

Writer and editor are not the same role. A writer generates from nothing. An editor directs from something. The creator economy flattened that distinction because the operating model was one person. AI has quietly made the distinction useful again.

I run a pipeline now that drafts the first pass of every issue. Python, Claude Code, GitHub cron, about a hundred lines of configuration encoding my voice and structure. Every Monday morning I give it a drop file with five news stories and a theme for the long read. By the time I sit down to work on the newsletter, the draft is already there.

Then I do the editorial work. Cut the weak paragraphs, sharpen the headlines, rewrite the sentences that sound generic, add the examples only I could add. Two hours instead of eight. Output quality up, not down.

I have just packaged the core pattern as a Claude Skill called `newsletter-editor`. It takes you through a seven-question setup on first run, capturing your niche, voice rules, section names, and length preference. From then on, every weekly drop file you feed it comes back as a full draft of both sections. News briefing plus thought leadership article. In your voice, not mine.

It is free. Open source. Works for any niche.

## Notes on which version to use

**Version 1 (short, link in comments)**: Better for reach. LinkedIn's algorithm still suppresses posts with external links in the body. Link in comments is the workaround and means more people see the post organically. Use this if the goal is awareness and list growth.

**Version 2 (longer, link in post)**: Better for conversion. Pre-qualifies the click. Anyone who reads to the end and clicks is warmer. Use this if the goal is skill installs from marketing-adjacent operators who are already following you.

I would run version 1 first. Then a week later, a follow-up post version 2 with a lessons-learned angle (what users said, what surprised you, what is coming next in the skill roadmap).

That gives you two posts off one launch, each doing a different job.
