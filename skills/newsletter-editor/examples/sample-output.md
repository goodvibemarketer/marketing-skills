# The Retrofit, 15 April 2026

## The Weekly Briefing

This week is about one shift settling in: the retrofit decision has moved out of sustainability teams and into finance committees, and the language changing first is a leading indicator of the capital flows changing second.

**The MEES 2030 consultation closed** with government confirming a minimum EPC C rating for commercial lets by 2027, then B by 2030, with penalties raised to £150k per breach. Most mid-sized commercial landlords still hold a meaningful slice of portfolio at D or E. The ones most exposed are private equity-owned portfolios where assets were sweated through the 2010s on the assumption that regulatory timelines would slip. The assumption has not held. The strategic read is that MEES is no longer a stick waved at a distant horizon; it is a dated compliance event inside current loan terms.

**British Land announced a £1.5bn retrofit programme** across its London estate, aligned to an SBTi 1.5 degree pathway, and took the earnings hit on guidance. The word worth noting in their announcement is not "leadership." It is "transition risk management." That is the register listed property now uses because that is the register their investors now use. When the biggest UK REITs stop calling retrofit climate action and start calling it risk management, mid-cap landlords are looking at the language that will shortly appear in their own analyst calls.

**TCFD reporting became mandatory for UK companies over 500 employees** from April, and the FCA clarified that a transition plan must be costed and scheduled, not aspirational. Auditors are already pushing back on vague wording. A leaked KPMG client note signalled that the big four will refuse sign-offs on unquantified transition plans. For property boards, this is the moment the climate paragraph in the annual report stops being a communications exercise and starts being a material disclosure.

**The Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant rose to £12k for non-domestic heat pumps**, but the Heat Pump Association has said plainly that installation capacity, not funding, is the binding constraint. Engineer availability, not capex, is what determines whether a 2027 deadline is hit. Landlords that start procurement now get installed. Landlords that wait until Q3 2026 find the installer diary full.

**The Green Finance Institute modelled £36bn of commercial property loans** due for 2027 refinancing that will fail the new MEES threshold without intervention. Lloyds has already started pricing the risk in, adding 75bps to loans on non-compliant buildings. That is the mechanism that actually moves landlords who were happy to take the MEES penalty on the chin. A fine is a one-off cost. A refinancing spread is permanent.

The pattern: the retrofit conversation has left the sustainability office and arrived at the CFO's desk. Regulation set the deadline. Debt markets set the urgency. Landlords who read this as an environmental issue are reading it off last year's map.

## The Long Read

Most landlords still think the retrofit question is about the planet.

It is not. Not anymore.

The planet argument has been on the table for twenty years, and for most of that time it lost to the spreadsheet. Now the spreadsheet has flipped.

I worked on my first serious retrofit in 2018. Seventies office block in Reading, tired plant, D-rated envelope, tenants grumbling about the summer heat. We ran the numbers. The payback was 12 years. The board wouldn't sign it off. I remember the finance director saying, almost apologetically, "it's a good project but the hurdle rate kills it." He wasn't wrong given the information he had.

Same building, same numbers, same board, today. Approved in one meeting. The project has not changed. The context has.

## The CFO Has Quietly Become the New Sustainability Officer

For two decades, the people pushing for retrofit inside commercial property businesses were in sustainability roles. They framed the argument in climate terms because that was what they knew and what their job title demanded. The argument was correct. It also lost almost every time, because the people making capital allocation decisions heard it as a cost centre pitch.

Something has shifted in the last eighteen months, and it is not that boards started caring about the climate. It is that the same argument is now being made by a different person, in a different register, with different numbers underneath it.

The CFO has picked up the file. The argument is the same argument, but framed as risk-weighted capital: MEES exposure as a compliance liability, refinancing spreads as a P&L item, EPC-linked rent premiums as a revenue line. The sustainability director's argument and the finance director's argument now point at the same action, but only one of them gets funded.

## The Green Premium Is Real and It Is Measurable

BNP Paribas has published data on London commercial rents showing A-rated space pulling roughly 8% higher rent than C-rated equivalents in comparable locations. Eight percent is not a rounding error. On a £2m annual rent roll, it is £160k of permanent uplift.

That number matters because it changes the business case from a cost-avoidance story to a revenue story. Cost avoidance is boring. Revenue is interesting. Boards sign off on revenue.

The mid-cap landlords who still treat retrofit as defensive spending are arguing against last year's framing. The offensive case exists and the data supports it. Whoever arrives at their investment committee next quarter without it will lose a decision they could have won.

## The Binding Constraint Is No Longer Capital

The old mental model was that retrofit was blocked by capital. If only the grants were higher, if only the payback were shorter, the projects would fly. So the sector lobbied for grants, and the grants came. The £12k BUS grant for non-domestic heat pumps is real money.

It has not unblocked the bottleneck.

The binding constraint now is installation capacity. There are not enough qualified heat pump engineers in the UK to install what the current pipeline needs, never mind the surge coming as 2027 deadlines approach. The Heat Pump Association says it plainly: they could install double what they are installing if the engineers existed.

That changes the strategy. If capital is the constraint, you wait for the price to come down. If capacity is the constraint, you book the slot. Landlords still treating this as a capital problem are queueing behind landlords who figured out the constraint changed six months ago.

## Debt Markets Are Faster Than Regulators

MEES was supposed to be the stick. The penalties for non-compliance would force the laggards into action.

In practice, the penalties were manageable. A £150k fine on a building worth £15m is an annoyance, not an emergency. Boards priced it, budgeted for it, and carried on.

The thing that actually moved the laggards was not the regulator. It was the bank. When Lloyds quietly started adding 75bps to refinancing on non-compliant buildings, the calculation changed overnight. A 75 basis point spread on a £200m loan portfolio is £1.5m a year, every year, in perpetuity. That makes retrofit not just economic but urgent.

The Green Finance Institute's £36bn refinancing exposure figure is not a forecast. It is a queue. Every loan in that queue resets in 2027, and every loan that resets without a valid EPC carries a pricing penalty that exceeds the retrofit capex. That is the mechanism the regulation was meant to create and failed to. The private market built it instead.

## What Changes Tomorrow Morning

The shift is not about doing the right thing. The shift is about doing the obvious thing before competitors figure out it is obvious.

Tomorrow morning, the question on the desk is not "can we afford to retrofit." It is "what does our portfolio look like at 2027 refinancing if we do not."

Most boards will still be arguing about the first question. A few will have moved on to the second. The gap between those two groups is the difference between the landlords who will own a premium portfolio in 2030 and the landlords who will be discounting their lease rates to fill buildings that have quietly become stranded assets.

The retrofit conversation left the boiler room years ago. It has now left the sustainability office. The only question left is whether it has reached your board.
