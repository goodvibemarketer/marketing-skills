# Voice Patterns

Generic voice rules applied to every newsletter draft, regardless of niche. These rules exist because they correlate strongly with writing that reads as human rather than AI-generated. Read this file alongside the drafting guides in Phase 2.

User-specific voice rules (stored in `newsletter-config.md`) override anything in this file where they conflict. Config wins.

## Paragraph length

Keep paragraphs short. One to three lines is the target. A paragraph of four lines is the upper limit. A paragraph of five or more lines almost always needs breaking up.

White space signals confidence. Dense blocks of prose signal corporate or academic writing, both of which are banned by default.

## Sentence rhythm

Alternate long and short. A 20-word sentence followed by a 6-word sentence reads better than two 13-word sentences. The contrast creates rhythm; the rhythm carries the reader.

Use standalone compression sentences to mark pivots. A compression sentence is 4–10 words, sits on its own line, and states the point:

> That changes the leverage.

> This is where it gets interesting.

> That distinction matters.

Do not overuse compression sentences. One or two per 300 words is the sweet spot. More than that becomes a tic.

## Direct sentences over hedged ones

Write:
- "This matters."
- "Here is the shift."
- "That is the real advantage."

Not:
- "It could be argued that this matters."
- "One might consider that there is a shift here."
- "There are many ways in which this could be seen as advantageous."

If a sentence sounds like it came from a consultant's invoice, rewrite it.

## Active voice

Active voice is the default. Passive voice is the exception, used only when the action matters more than the actor.

Write:
- "AI exposes gaps in thinking."
- "Most teams skip the setup phase."

Not:
- "Gaps in thinking are exposed by AI."
- "The setup phase is skipped by most teams."

## Banned language (default)

Strip the following from every draft unless the config explicitly allows them:

- **Hype words**: game-changing, revolutionary, next-gen, unlock, seamless, unprecedented, unparalleled, cutting-edge
- **Consultant jargon**: synergy, enablement, best-in-class, robust framework (unless truly warranted), scalable solutions, mission-critical
- **Hedging phrases**: it could be argued, one might consider, there are many ways in which, some would say
- **Transition cliches**: in today's fast-paced world, now more than ever, in the age of AI

If a normal human would not say it out loud in conversation, do not write it.

## Em dashes

By default, em dashes are banned. Replace with:
- A full stop (for a hard break)
- A comma (for a soft break)
- A colon (if introducing a definition or list)
- Two short sentences (often the best option)

If the config explicitly allows em dashes, respect that. Otherwise, banned.

## Rhetorical questions

Used sparingly, rhetorical questions are useful for guiding the reader's thinking:
- "Why does this matter now?"
- "What actually changes?"

Used liberally, they become a tic. One or two per article. Zero in the news section.

## Rhetorical device budget

Assume a total budget across the whole article:
- Compression sentences: 4–6 across a 1,500-word article
- Rhetorical questions: 1–2 across a 1,500-word article
- Named frameworks or patterns: 0–1 across a 1,500-word article

Exceeding the budget makes the writing feel artificial. Staying under it keeps the prose grounded.

## First-person cues

When the config requires first-person (most do), use natural anchors:
- "In my experience..."
- "I built [X] and..."
- "I spent the last [time period] [doing thing]..."
- "What I noticed..."

Do not say "in my humble opinion" or "I just want to say." These signal weakness and add nothing.

## Opinionated section headings (article section only)

Section headings take a position. They make a claim the section then supports.

Write:
- "Most Marketing Teams Don't Have an AI Problem. They Have a Clarity Problem."
- "AI Does Not Intuit Brand. It Responds to Articulation."

Not:
- "The AI Problem in Marketing"
- "Brand and AI"

A good heading could stand alone as a tweet. A bad heading is a topic label.

## What voice is not

- Voice is not just word choice. It is rhythm, structure, and commitment.
- Voice is not consistency of vocabulary. It is consistency of point of view.
- Voice is not the absence of jargon. It is the deliberate use of jargon where it earns its place.

Apply these rules mechanically when drafting. Then read the draft back and ask: does this sound like a specific person thinking on the page, or does it sound like content? If the second, cut, tighten, and sharpen until the first.
